The Bearing edit

Ten dahabiyas. Never more than 24 guests.

Most visitors to Egypt see the Nile from a coach window or a 300-passenger cruise ship. Nour El Nil is the opposite of all that. A fleet of ten dahabiyas — traditional wooden sailing vessels — each carrying a maximum of 24 guests, drifting between Luxor and Aswan at the pace of the wind itself. That is not a typo. Twenty-four guests across the entire boat. Not per deck.


This is how 19th-century travellers — Agatha Christie, Florence Nightingale, Gustave Flaubert — experienced Egypt. The temples appear from the riverbank without warning. Meals are laid on the upper deck as the sun goes down over the Valley of the Kings. No announcements. No schedules. No crowds.


Each vessel is constructed from the keel up by Memdouh, a sailor with over three decades on this river, and finished by Eleonore, a Parisian interior designer whose hand is visible in the whitewashed joinery, hand-woven textiles, and warm amber light of every cabin. The top deck is the living room: open to the desert air, strung with hammocks, shaded by a canvas awning, the teakwood planking warm underfoot by mid-morning. Cabins run panoramic to standard — the panoramics offer floor-to-ceiling sliding glass that frames the east bank in the blue hour before dawn. Propulsion is wind-first; a small escort motorboat stands by for calms. The sound signature is water on hull and cotton sail under tension. Sites along the line — Gebel Silsileh, El Kab, the sandstone quarries of Horemheb — are accessed exclusively by this class of vessel; larger ships are prohibited by draft and regulation. Meals come full-board, sourced daily from farmers and fishermen at each mooring point.

"We anchored at Edfu at dusk. There were no other boats. The temple was ours for an hour before sunrise. Nothing I have seen in thirty years of travel compares."

— The Bearing Editor, January 2025

6 days · 5 nights · 250 km

Esna to Aswan — the full route

1
Day one · Esna
Embarkation & the Temple of Khnum
Esna, 33 miles south of Luxor, is where you board your dahabiya and meet the crew. A thriving merchant town, it is best known for the small ancient Temple of Khnum — which sits 9 metres below street level, as if the modern city grew up around and over it. Your journey begins with a walk to the temple and a stroll through streets filled with beautiful textiles and exotic goods.
2
Day two · El Kab & Edfu
Necropolis, horse-drawn carriages & Horus
El Kab, on the East Bank, was once the capital of Upper Egypt and remains home to the temple of Nekhbet and a row of decorated tombs carved into the mountain slope. You'll walk through a small fishing village to reach the necropolis — unhurried, with almost no one else around. In the afternoon the boat arrives at Edfu, home to the Greco-Roman temple dedicated to the god Horus — among the best preserved in all of Egypt. Horse and carriage takes you through the busy city streets to reach it. After returning to the boat, you set sail for a beautiful island anchorage for the night.
3
Day three · Under sail
Sail, swim & hieroglyphs in the desert
A day entirely devoted to the river. After an optional morning walk and breakfast, you set sail. In the afternoon, the boat stops at an island for swimming in the Nile. As dusk approaches, there may be an opportunity to walk into the desert along a valley lined with boulders covered in ancient hieroglyphs and stone-age inscriptions — the beginning of the shortest trail through the desert to Libya (28 days in ancient times). At night, the boat ties up at the foot of the floodlit temple of Gebel Silsileh.
4
Day four · Gebel Silsileh
The narrowest point of the Nile
Here, the Nile flows between two mountain ranges at its narrowest point on the entire river. Large cruise ships are not permitted to stop — which means you will almost certainly be the only visitors. After exploring the small temple of Horemheb and walking through the sandstone quarries where ancient Egyptians cut stone for their most famous monuments, you sail for the remainder of the day to a picturesque riverbank for lunch and swimming.
5
Day five · Kom Ombo
The crocodile temple & the mummy museum
After a night on the water beneath the stars, the morning brings you to the temple of Kom Ombo — built during the Ptolemaic period on a dramatic bend in the river, dedicated to Sobek, the crocodile-headed god. The location is among the most beautiful of any temple on the Nile. Afterwards, visit the Crocodile Museum, where dozens of mummified crocodiles attest to the creature's ancient importance in this region.
6
Day six · Aswan
Disembarkation
Wake to the sight of the desert closing in on the river at Aswan Bridge. Your journey ends here — relaxed, unhurried, and with the particular quietness that only comes from six days at the pace of the wind. Disembarkation takes place in the morning. Note: Egyptian authorities require all dahabiyas to overnight in the dock beside Aswan Bridge. This is a legal requirement, and somewhat less romantic than the preceding nights — but the rest more than makes up for it.
A note on flexibility: Nour El Nil reserves the right to vary the itinerary as conditions require. The Nile's moods and Egyptian river authority regulations sometimes mean changes are announced only hours in advance. What never changes is the spirit of the journey.
Practical details

Know before you go

Best season
October to April. Summer temperatures regularly exceed 45°C — avoid June through August if you can.
Getting there
Fly into Luxor (LXR) direct from Cairo, or via London, Frankfurt or Dubai. The cruise begins in Esna, 33 miles south — transfer from your Luxor hotel is included.
Visa
Most nationalities get a 30-day visa on arrival at Egyptian airports, or apply in advance via e-visa for around $25 USD. Check your country's requirements before travelling.
Currency
Egyptian pounds (EGP). USD and EUR are widely accepted at tourist sites and markets. Bring small bills — ATMs in Aswan work well, but cash is king along the river.
Seasickness
Not a concern. The Nile has no waves or swell — it is a flat, calm river. No guest has ever reported feeling unwell from the motion.
What to pack
Light layers for days, something warm for evenings on deck. Modest clothing for temple visits (covered shoulders and knees). Good walking shoes — temple floors are uneven.
Tipping
Expected but never pressured. Budget around $10–15 USD per person per day across crew and guides. Bring small USD bills — $1s and $5s are the most useful.
Health
No mandatory vaccinations, though Hepatitis A and Typhoid are commonly recommended. Drink only bottled water. Sun protection is essential — the light on the river is intense.
